# Business AI Workshop Design

Design short, outcome-oriented AI/AX workshops for owners and executives. The workshop must leave each participant with a selected business problem, a safe human-reviewed workflow, and a time-bounded experiment—not merely awareness of tools.

## When to Use

Use this skill when asked to design:

- a 1–4 hour AI or AX class for owners, executives, SMEs, manufacturers, or merchants
- a mixed-industry workshop that needs a common core and segment-specific examples
- a workshop that should lead ethically into diagnosis, consulting, or a pilot
- curriculum, agenda, exercises, facilitator notes, worksheets, and evaluation for business audiences

Do not use it for deep developer training, model engineering, or a presentation with no participant practice.

## Required Inputs

Capture or infer only low-risk defaults:

- audience roles, industries, AI literacy, and expected headcount
- duration and delivery mode
- business outcome the sponsor expects
- device, account, network, and room constraints
- sensitive data classes and tool policy
- desired post-course action and any commercial follow-up boundary

Ask only when missing information would materially change safety, delivery mode, or commitment. Otherwise draft a complete default and label assumptions.

## Procedure

### 1. Define one observable exit outcome

Write a promise in this form:

> By the end, each participant will select one business task, design a safe AI-assisted workflow, and complete a measured experiment plan.

Avoid outcomes such as “understand AI” unless paired with a produced artifact. Completion criterion: the outcome names a participant-created artifact and a next action.

### 2. Translate AX into executive economics

Frame the topic around revenue leakage, cost, cycle time, quality, risk, and owner intervention time. Define AX as redesigning work around AI with accountable human decisions—not subscribing to a tool.

Completion criterion: every case maps to at least one business metric.

### 3. Segment mixed audiences without fragmenting the course

Use a common decision framework and branch only cases, worksheets, and discussion prompts. Default to roughly 70% common core and 30% segment-specific material. If pre-survey results show one segment exceeds about 70%, increase that segment’s examples rather than keeping artificial balance.

Completion criterion: all participants share the same learning objectives while seeing at least two relevant examples for their segment.

### 4. Build backward from participant artifacts

A two-hour default should produce:

1. an inventory of 2–3 recurring tasks
2. a scored priority task
3. an AI workflow canvas showing input, AI step, output, human review, prohibited data, and quality criteria
4. a prompt or instruction draft
5. a 2-week prototype inside a 30-day measured experiment

Allocate at least one-third of live time to diagnosis, design, practice, and commitment. Limit product demonstration to one deep common example; use short micro-cases for breadth.

### 5. Select safe starter problems

Prefer tasks that are frequent, time-consuming, structured, and easy for a person to verify: summarization, classification, drafting, comparison, checklist creation, and standardization.

Do not promise predictive maintenance, demand forecasting, autonomous quality decisions, dynamic pricing, customer compensation, or safety decisions without verified data, controls, and domain review.

Completion criterion: each recommended starter task has a named reviewer and a reversible failure mode.

### 6. Design for operational failure

Provide dummy or de-identified data. Never require participants to paste customer data, contracts, drawings, source documents, costs, personnel records, or unpublished production information into public AI tools.

Prepare:

- instructor-led demonstration that does not depend on participant accounts
- printable worksheets and mobile-friendly participation
- screenshots or a short backup recording for network/tool failure
- a non-tool path that still achieves the learning objectives

Completion criterion: the workshop can continue if Wi-Fi, sign-up, or the demonstration tool fails.

### 7. Use a business prompt structure

Teach a reusable request pattern:

- role
- task and business purpose
- context/input
- procedure
- constraints and prohibited behavior
- output format
- verification criteria
- “mark unknowns as confirmation needed; do not guess”

The prompt is part of the workflow, not the whole workflow. Always identify the human decision point.

### 8. Design measurement and follow-up

Use one or two metrics only: handling time, rework, omissions, response time, conversion, or owner intervention time. Record the baseline before the prototype. Add an explicit stop, revise, or expand decision at day 30.

If commercial follow-up is appropriate, keep it opt-in and short:

1. fit check
2. opportunity diagnosis
3. workflow design
4. one-task pilot
5. scale only after measured validation

Judge success by qualified problems and viable pilots, not raw consultation sign-ups. Keep the sales segment under 10% of class time.

### 9. Reconcile specialist reviews

For important programs, gather independent views from research/market, business development, and instructional design specialists. Record disagreements and make explicit decisions, especially around:

- broad showcase versus deep practice
- separate tracks versus one mixed cohort
- rapid prototype duration versus full measurement window
- education value versus lead generation

Completion criterion: the final brief names the decision and rationale rather than pasting three parallel opinions.

## Deliverable Structure

1. recommendation and title
2. audience and assumptions
3. observable learning objectives
4. minute-by-minute agenda with methods and outputs
5. common and segment-specific cases
6. exercises and worksheets
7. prompt examples
8. privacy and human-review rules
9. evaluation and 30-day measurement
10. materials and contingency plan
11. optional follow-up ladder
12. risks, mitigations, and specialist decisions
13. cited official sources for any current regional or policy claims

For a worked two-hour AX pattern, read `references/two-hour-ax-blueprint.md`.

## Pitfalls

- **Tool tour disguised as transformation:** participants leave impressed but unable to act.
- **One generic case for a mixed cohort:** neither segment sees its own economics.
- **Too many demos:** breadth crowds out diagnosis and practice.
- **Actual business data in exercises:** creates avoidable confidentiality and privacy exposure.
- **Unverified savings claims:** promise measurement, not a percentage reduction.
- **A consulting pitch at the end:** damages trust and lowers learning transfer.
- **Agenda minutes that do not sum exactly:** calculate and verify the total.
- **Specialist outputs copied without reconciliation:** surface conflicts and make one final design choice.

## Verification

Before delivery, verify:

- agenda minutes equal the requested duration
- at least one-third of time is active participant work
- each learning objective maps to an agenda activity and artifact
- each starter use case has human review and prohibited-data guidance
- the workshop still works without participant accounts or live internet
- the final artifact contains a baseline, KPI, owner, review date, and stop/revise/expand rule
- current factual claims have official citations and checked dates
- follow-up is optional, proportionate, and not represented as guaranteed funding or guaranteed ROI
