# Instructional Design for Practical AI Training

Design repeatable training products that lead to observable workplace performance, not feature awareness. Use this skill for lectures, workshops, bootcamps, customer-customized courses, facilitator guides, exercises, evaluations, and pilot plans.

## Core Standard

A course is successful when learners can produce or perform something relevant to work. Start with the behavior and artifact, then select only the concepts and tool features needed to achieve it.

## Procedure

1. **Frame the request.** Identify audience, role, current skill, duration, environment, desired output, deadline, and customer constraints. If information is missing but a safe default exists, state assumptions and draft immediately; reserve questions for choices that materially change the design.
2. **Separate product layers.** Distinguish the reusable standard module from customer-specific examples, policies, terminology, and data. Never place identifiable customer information in examples or external AI services without approval.
3. **Write observable outcomes.** Use verbs such as identify, decompose, write, run, compare, test, revise, classify, and justify. Avoid outcomes limited to “understand” or “learn.”
4. **Choose the capstone artifact.** Define what each learner will leave with: a prompt, workflow map, prototype, validation record, automation proposal, or implementation plan.
5. **Design backward.** Map each outcome to a practice activity and an assessment criterion. Remove content that does not support an outcome or artifact.
6. **Fit the clock.** Calculate the total duration and the explanation/practice ratio. For beginner practical courses, prefer demonstration, guided practice, independent practice, feedback, and assessment over extended tool tours. Explain why the ratio fits the goal and audience.
7. **Build a learning progression.** Use: explain → demonstrate → guided attempt → independent attempt → feedback → transfer to work. Let learners encounter a baseline limitation before adding rules, tests, or automation safeguards.
8. **Teach verification and human review.** Define normal and error cases, evidence of success, review points, security/privacy boundaries, and what may not be deployed or shared without organizational approval.
9. **Prepare failure paths.** Supply example outputs, offline or low-dependency alternatives, pre-downloaded files, and facilitator recovery instructions for login, network, installation, quota, or device failures.
10. **Create the complete draft set.** Prepare a course plan, slides outline, facilitator script, learner worksheet, prompt examples, pre-assessment, post-assessment, satisfaction survey, readiness checklist, timed run sheet, anticipated Q&A, and fallback activity.
11. **Ground changing facts.** Prefer official product documentation for features, pricing, limits, permissions, and security. Add source and check date. Mark unverified functions as assumptions and test critical procedures before delivery.
12. **Protect copyright.** When inspired by third-party training material, extract principles rather than copying slides, diagrams, prompts, screenshots, exercises, or code. Record page-level citations, inspect the source’s rights notice, create original examples and visuals, and do not redistribute the source unless permitted.
13. **Pilot and improve.** Define success criteria before delivery: satisfaction, pre/post gain, completion rate, artifact quality, reuse rate, facilitator preparation time, and follow-on consulting demand. Preserve unfavorable data and distinguish observed facts from hypotheses.

## Two-Hour Practical Course Pattern

Use this only as a starting point and recalculate for each course:

- Opening and diagnostic
- Essential concept and risk boundary
- Instructor demonstration
- Guided build or workflow practice
- Verification and security/privacy check
- Independent workplace transfer activity
- Post-assessment and next action

A two-hour course should teach one coherent performance loop, not compress an advanced curriculum into a lecture. Offer advanced topics as a separate workshop or project course.

## Product Ladder Pattern

When demand is not yet validated, use:

1. **Introductory lecture/workshop:** one small output and one verification loop.
2. **Applied workshop:** role-specific scenario, requirements, implementation, testing, and feedback.
3. **Team project course:** shared prototype, operating rules, review gates, and adoption plan.

Pilot the smallest viable course first, then expand using completion evidence and learner work products—not topic popularity alone.

## Assessment Design

### Pre-assessment

Measure prior tool access, task familiarity, confidence, and prerequisite actions. Include one diagnostic performance item where possible.

### Post-assessment

Score the learner artifact with a short rubric. Typical dimensions:

- Problem and user are defined.
- Inputs, outputs, and completion conditions are explicit.
- The learner executed the procedure or prototype.
- Normal and error cases were checked.
- Human review and data-safety points are identified.

Set the completion threshold in advance and report both completion and non-completion honestly.

## Customer Intake Checklist

- Industry and organization type
- Departments and job roles
- Participant count and proficiency distribution
- Allowed AI tools, accounts, licenses, and quotas
- Security, privacy, and data residency rules
- Real work problems learners want to address
- Venue, devices, OS, installation rights, browser, network, and firewall constraints
- Whether learners may use personal devices/accounts
- Delivery format, available support staff, accessibility needs, and time constraints

## Deliverable Quality Gate

Before declaring a draft complete, verify:

- Every outcome is observable.
- Every outcome has a practice or assessment.
- Time allocations sum exactly to the advertised duration.
- The exercise can be completed by the least-experienced intended learner.
- Normal and failure paths are both prepared.
- Human review, privacy, security, and deployment boundaries are visible.
- Product facts have current official sources and check dates.
- Third-party intellectual property is cited but not reproduced without permission.
- The standard reusable module and customer customization are clearly separated.
- Any external distribution, pricing, scope, schedule, or customer-specific example has the required approval.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature-tour overload:** learners remember menus but cannot perform work. Cut features and preserve the performance loop.
- **Unobservable objectives:** “understand AI” cannot be assessed. Replace it with a visible action or artifact.
- **One-shot success theater:** an AI-generated output is not evidence of competence. Require execution, an error case, and revision.
- **Production implications in an intro course:** explicitly separate personal prototype, internal review, and production deployment.
- **Real customer data in exercises:** use synthetic or irreversibly anonymized data.
- **Copied competitor courseware:** reference concepts and page ranges, but author original structure, examples, visuals, prompts, and assessments.
- **Unverified current features:** a recent-looking slide deck is not an official product source. Recheck before teaching.
- **Overbuilt first release:** validate a short flagship course before producing a large catalog.

## Verification

- Recalculate all time totals and percentages with a calculation tool.
- Run the critical learner procedure in the actual delivery environment.
- Test at least one failure case and the fallback activity.
- Review sample data for personal, customer, contractual, HR, payroll, or confidential information.
- Confirm citations resolve and page references match the extracted source.
- Conduct an internal rehearsal and record facilitator friction before the pilot.
